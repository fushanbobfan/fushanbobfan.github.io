whirligig

A tilted top that is not spinning falls straight over. Spin it, and gravity's pull no longer topples it: the spin turns that pull into a slow sideways swing, so the axle goes round in a cone. Let go of a spinning top and it does fall a little at first, and the falling itself is what swings it sideways; it bobs back up, again and again, while it goes round. The faster it spins, the smaller that nod and the slower the swing. Below is a toy top worked out from Newton's laws for a spinning body.

As the spin dies

With friction at the tip the spin slowly runs down. A slower spin needs a faster swing round to hold the top up, so the precession speeds up and the nod grows. A top standing nearly upright stays upright until its spin drops below the rate it needs to stand, about 1,136 rpm for this one, and then wobbles outwards. In the end the rim meets the table. Real tops also climb back towards upright while they spin fast, because their tip slides on the table; this tip stays put, so that part is missing.

Why it cannot fall

Without friction, two things stay fixed while the top moves: its spin about the axle, and its angular momentum about the vertical. With both fixed, each tilt comes with its own rate of swinging round, and with the energy that swing needs. Beyond some tilt the swing needs more energy than the fall has released, so the tilt is walled in. The curve is the energy the nod needs at each tilt; the top can only lean where the curve is below its energy, the shaded band. A slow spin lowers the outer wall until it lies past the point where the rim meets the table.

The three shapes

Let go from rest, the axle starts with no sideways motion, so at the top of every nod it stops dead for an instant: the tip draws cusps. Give it a push in the direction it would go anyway and it never stops: waves. Push it the other way and for part of each nod it runs backwards: loops. Push it at exactly the steady rate and the nod vanishes, leaving a smooth cone.
