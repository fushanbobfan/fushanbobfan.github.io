The Moon pulls on the whole Earth, but not equally: the side facing it is pulled a little harder than the centre, and the centre a little harder than the far side. Take away the pull on the centre, which only keeps the Earth and the Moon going round each other, and what is left stretches the oceans along the line to the Moon. That is why the sea rises twice a day, on the side under the Moon and on the side away from it.

Two months of tides

The curve is the height of the tide at a point on the equator, with the real Moon and Sun of the dates shown: two high waters a day, swelling to spring tides at new (●) and full (○) moon and shrinking to neap tides in between. P and A mark the Moon at its nearest (perigee) and farthest (apogee); a shaded band is a perigean spring tide, when perigee falls within a day and a half of new or full moon and spring tides are at their largest. Why the far side bulges too

With the Sun off, switch the arrows to the Moon's pull. Every arrow points at the Moon and they look the same length, because the Moon is 60 Earth radii away and the Earth's near and far sides are less than 2 percent closer or farther. That pull keeps the whole Earth falling round the centre of mass it shares with the Moon, and it moves the oceans exactly as much as the rock.

Switch back. With the pull on the centre taken away, the near side is left with a small pull towards the Moon and the far side with a small pull away from it: the far side is simply falling towards the Moon less than the Earth's centre does. Halfway round, the leftover points inward, at half the strength. The oceans are pulled out at both ends of the line to the Moon and pressed in round the middle.

The leftover falls off with the cube of the distance, so moving the Moon from its farthest to its nearest point raises the tide by about 40 percent, while its plain pull grows by only a quarter. The Sun pulls on the Earth about 180 times harder than the Moon, but it is 390 times farther away, so its tide comes out a little under half of the Moon's.
