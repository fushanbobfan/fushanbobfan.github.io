shutterwarp

Most phone cameras take a picture one line of pixels at a time. Each line is exposed a moment after the one before it, and the sweep across the whole frame takes something like a hundredth to a thirtieth of a second. A propeller turning thousands of times a minute moves a long way in that time, so every line catches the blades in a different place. Put the lines together and the straight blades come out bent, lopsided, or broken into pieces.

What the camera is doing

The sensor in a phone is a grid of light-sensitive pixels. With a rolling shutter, each line of pixels starts and stops collecting light a little later than the line before it, so the lines record the scene at staggered moments. A global shutter starts and stops every pixel together; it needs extra circuitry in each pixel, which is why most phone sensors roll. For anything that moves slowly compared with the sweep, the stagger makes no visible difference. A propeller is the classic case where it does: the tip of a blade on a small aircraft travels hundreds of metres a second.

This page scans from the top of the frame to the bottom. In a real camera the direction depends on how the sensor sits in the body, so turning the phone on its side turns the sweep too. The page also gives every row an instant exposure, as a very short shutter speed in bright light would; a longer exposure smears each row along the blade's path and adds motion blur on top of the bending.

Each row of the photo is then a true picture of a thin strip of the scene, taken at its own moment. The strips come from different instants, which is why the shapes in the photo can look like no propeller ever built.
