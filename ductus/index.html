ductus

A Bézier curve is nothing but repeated in-between points. Take each leg of the control polygon, mark the point a fraction t of the way along it, join those points, and do it again until one point is left: that point is on the curve. Drag the control points and slide t.

Reading the picture

  • The construction. Each coloured level is one round of in-between points. A curve with n + 1 control points needs n rounds. The last segment drawn touches the curve: it is the tangent, and its length times n is the speed of the moving point.
  • The curvature comb. Each tooth stands on the curve, at right angles to it, with a length proportional to the curvature there: one over the radius of the circle that best fits the curve at that point. Long teeth mark tight turns; where the teeth cross to the other side, the curve inflects.
  • The two halves. The outer edges of the construction are themselves control polygons: the left edge draws the curve up to t, the right edge the rest. Splitting this way is how drawing programs cut a curve without changing its shape.
  • Steps of t and of length. Round dots mark t = 0, 0.1, …, 1; bars across the curve cut it into ten pieces of equal length. The dots bunch up where the point moves slowly and spread out where it is fast, so animating t at a steady rate does not move along the curve at a steady pace. Play at constant speed steps through length instead, finding each t from a table of lengths refined with Newton's method.
  • Weights. Give each control point a weight and do the construction one dimension up: lift each point to (x·w, y·w, w), interpolate as before, then divide by the last coordinate. The result is a rational Bézier curve. A quadratic with end weights 1 is a parabola when the middle weight is 1, a piece of an ellipse below 1 and of a hyperbola above; with equal legs and the middle weight equal to the cosine of half the arc's angle, it is an exact circle.

Lengths are in board units; the board is 600 units across.
