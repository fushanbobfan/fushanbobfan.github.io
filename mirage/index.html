mirage

On a hot day the road far ahead often looks wet, and cars seem to drive through a puddle that is never there when you arrive. The asphalt heats a thin layer of air above it, and light travels slightly faster through hot air than through cool air. A ray from the sky heading for the road at a shallow angle curves back up through that layer and reaches your eye from below. What you see on the road is a piece of the sky.

Where each ray lands

Every row of the view above is one ray from the eye. The left chart follows those rows from the top of the view to the bottom and marks how high each ray is when it reaches the board. Without heating the height falls steadily: one upright board, then road. Over a hot road the rays aimed lowest dip into the hot air and climb out again, so below a certain row the height starts to rise. That turning point is the fold. Rows below it meet the board from the bottom up, which is the upside-down copy, and nothing lower than the fold is seen at all.

Why hot air bends light

Light slows down a little in air, by about three parts in ten thousand at freezing point. The slowing grows with the density of the air, and hot air is thinner, so light runs a little faster just above a hot road than at the height of your eyes. A ray crossing from slower air into faster air at a shallow angle bends away from the faster side, the way a line of marchers wheels when one end steps onto firmer ground. Heading down into the hot layer, a ray bends ever flatter until it runs level and then climbs out again.

Only rays that meet the layer at a very shallow angle turn back: the difference in speed is tiny, so the steepest ray that still turns is aimed a fraction of a degree below eye level. That is why the shine always lies in a thin band near the horizon, and why it keeps its distance as you drive towards it: the band is fixed by angle, and the stretch of road seen at that angle moves with you.

Among the rays that dip through the layer, the ones from higher up the board reach your eye from lower down, so the copy under the board is upside down. The hot layer works like a mirror lying on the road, but a soft one: it often shows only the lowest part of what stands in it, and it shimmers because the hot air above real asphalt rises in restless plumes.

The page uses a smooth layer whose temperature falls from the road value to the air value over a few times its thickness, with the refractive index of dry air for visible light. The ground is flat; over a few hundred metres the curve of the Earth lowers it by millimetres.
