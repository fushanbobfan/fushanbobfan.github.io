syzygy

The Moon passes between the Earth and the Sun every month, yet a solar eclipse comes only a few times a year. Its orbit is tilted about 5° to the Earth's, so most new moons pass above or below the Sun and most full moons miss the Earth's shadow. Eclipses happen only when a new or full moon falls near one of the two places where the orbits cross.

This is a made-up tilt: the chart and the list show what would happen. The real Moon is tilted 5.1°.

The year at a glance

The curve is the Moon's height above or below the Sun's path in the sky. Solid dots are new moons and orange rings full moons; the bar through each is how close it has to come to the line for an eclipse, and the moons that make it are drawn larger. Shaded spans are eclipse seasons. Click to move the date.

From above

Play the year and watch the line where the orbits cross. It keeps pointing the same way in space while the Earth goes round the Sun, so twice a year it points at the Sun. Those are the eclipse seasons. The line turns slowly backwards, once in 18.6 years, so the seasons come about 19 days earlier each year.
