Thread a few beads on a taut string and pluck it. However tangled the motion looks, it is a sum of a few simple patterns, the normal modes, each swinging at its own steady frequency. Drag a bead to pluck the string, or click a bar to play one mode alone.

Reading the picture

  • The equation. Each bead is pulled toward its two neighbours by the string and, if the anchor is on, back toward the axis by a spring of its own: yj″ = c²(yj−1 − 2yj + yj+1) − a² yj − γ yj′, with the walls held at zero. c is the coupling, a the anchor and γ the damping.
  • Normal modes. With n beads there are exactly n patterns in which every bead swings at one shared frequency: sine-shaped arches with 1, 2, …, n humps. Any motion at all is a mix of them, and with no damping each mode keeps its energy forever, so the bars do not change while the string writhes.
  • Why beads bend the overtones. On a smooth string the k-th mode swings exactly k times as fast as the first, which is why a guitar string sounds a clean note. On a beaded string the frequency is 2c sin(kπ / 2(n+1)): close to k times the first for long waves, then falling behind and levelling off below 2c as the waves shrink toward the bead spacing. The readout compares the first overtones with the whole numbers a smooth string would give.
  • Plucking and striking. A pluck starts the string bent and still; a strike starts it straight with one bead moving. A strike at one bead gives mode k a share of energy proportional to the square of that mode's height at the bead, whatever its frequency, so on average the high modes get as much as the low ones. A plucked triangle is smooth everywhere except its corner, so it contains little of the tightly wiggling high modes, and their energy falls off quickly with k. That is why a hammered string sounds brighter than a plucked one.
  • Anchors. Hanging each bead on its own spring turns the string into a row of pendulums joined by springs. Every frequency rises to √(a² + 4c² sin²(kπ / 2(n+1))), so even the longest wave cannot swing slower than a. Waves of different lengths now travel at very different speeds, which is why a pulse on the pendulums frays.
  • Beats. With two beads, weak coupling and a strong anchor, the two modes have nearly equal frequencies. Starting one bead alone mixes both modes; as they drift in and out of step, the motion passes to the other bead and back once every 2π / (ω₂ − ω₁).
  • Damping. A drag proportional to each bead's speed removes energy from every mode that still swings at the same average rate, the damping γ, so the energy halves every ln 2 / γ time units. A mode whose frequency is below γ / 2 no longer swings at all; it creeps back to the line, and the slower it is, the longer it takes.
