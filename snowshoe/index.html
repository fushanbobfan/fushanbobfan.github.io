Reading the picture

  • The equations. Prey x grow at rate r and are caught at rate a·x·y; a share e of what is caught becomes new predators y, which die at rate m: x′ = r x − a x y, y′ = e a x y − m y.
  • The phase plane. Every point is a pair of populations, and the small arrows show which way that pair moves. An orbit is the path one population traces. Time is not drawn here; the panel below shows it.
  • The dashed lines. On the horizontal one the prey stop changing (just enough predators to eat every newborn), and on the vertical one the predators stop changing (just enough prey to replace the dead). Where they cross is the balance point: x = m / (e a), y = r / a. Orbits turn counter-clockwise around it.
  • Closed loops. The quantity V = e a x − m ln x + a y − r ln y never changes along an orbit, so each population returns exactly to where it started and loops forever. The readout shows how far the numerical solution lets V drift.
  • Averages. However wild the swings, the populations averaged over one full cycle equal the balance point. Volterra built on this to explain why predatory fish made up a larger share of the Adriatic catch when fishing fell during the First World War.
  • Fishing. Effort f removes the same share of both populations, which is the same as lowering the prey birth rate to r − f and raising the predator death rate to m + f. The balance point moves to more prey and fewer predators, so a pause in fishing favours the predators. Once f reaches r the predators cannot be sustained at all.
