Thousands of drivers go from S to T, and each one takes whichever route is fastest for them. Open a free shortcut and every one of them can arrive later than before. Nobody makes a mistake: each driver's choice is the best one given what everyone else does, and together those choices make every trip slower.

How it works

Each road has a rule for how long it takes. A road marked x/100 takes one minute for every hundred cars on it: quick when empty, slow at rush hour. A road marked 45 takes 45 minutes however many cars use it, like a long highway that never fills. The free link takes no time at all.

In Braess's network 4000 drivers split evenly between the two routes, 2000 each, and every trip takes 20 + 45 = 65 minutes. Open the link from A to B. A driver at S now sees that S → A takes 20 minutes and the free link plus B → T takes 20 more: 40 minutes beats 45, so they switch. So does everyone else, until all 4000 drivers are on both crowded roads and each trip takes 40 + 0 + 40 = 80 minutes.

Nobody wants to go back. The old routes now take 40 + 45 = 85 minutes, worse than 80. This is an equilibrium, named for John Glen Wardrop, who stated it for traffic in 1952: every route in use takes the same time, and no unused route is faster. No single driver can gain by changing route, even though all of them would gain if the link were closed again.

The day-by-day replay shows how drivers could find the equilibrium without anyone planning it. Each morning, on every route slower than the fastest, a share of the drivers switches to the fastest one, larger the more time they would save. When drivers switch too readily they overshoot: the new favourite fills up and is slower the next day than the route they left.

The best split for everyone is the one that makes the total time spent on the road smallest. It differs from the equilibrium because a driver choosing a route counts only their own trip time and not the delay they add to everyone else on a crowded road. Charge each driver that extra delay as a toll, and the drivers' own choices land on the best split.

The paradox needs the right amount of traffic. With few drivers the link really helps, because the crowded roads are nearly empty. With very many drivers nobody wants the link, because the crowded roads are slower than the fixed ones. In Braess's network it hurts for anything between 3000 and 9000 drivers, which the chart shades.

How bad can choosing alone get? Tim Roughgarden and Éva Tardos proved in 2002 that when every road's time grows in a straight line with its traffic, as here, the total time at equilibrium is never more than 4/3 of the best possible. Pigou's two roads reach that bound exactly at 6000 drivers.

Dietrich Braess published the example in 1968. Planners have since pointed to cases where closing a road did not make traffic worse, such as the removal of the Cheonggyecheon elevated highway in Seoul, begun in 2003. Joel Cohen and Paul Horowitz showed in 1991 that the same paradox appears in networks of springs and strings: cut a string, and the weight it holds can rise.
