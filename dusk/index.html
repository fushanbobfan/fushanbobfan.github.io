dusk

Sunlight is a mix of every colour. On its way through the air, the molecules knock a little of it sideways, and they knock blue light about five times as often as red. That scattered blue, coming at you from every part of the sky, is the blue sky. When the sun is low its light crosses dozens of times more air before it reaches you, so most of the blue has been scattered out of the beam by the time it arrives, and the sun and the clouds it lights turn orange and red. Every pixel below is worked out from that scattering, one wavelength at a time.

What is in the model, and what is not

The air thins out with height, and the Earth is round, so a low sun's light skims a long way through the lower air. Molecules scatter light by the inverse fourth power of its wavelength; haze scatters it more evenly and mostly forwards, which is why a hazy sky is whiter and brightest around the sun. The ozone layer, 10 to 40 km up, absorbs a little orange light: by day you hardly notice, but after sunset the sunlight reaching the sky overhead has skimmed through it for hundreds of kilometres, and that is what keeps the twilight sky blue.

Each ray of sunlight is scattered at most once before it reaches you. Light scattered two or more times brightens the sky near the horizon and the twilight sky, so both come out darker here than they really are. There are no clouds and no bending of light near the horizon, and the sun's light above the air is taken to be that of a body glowing at 5778 K.
